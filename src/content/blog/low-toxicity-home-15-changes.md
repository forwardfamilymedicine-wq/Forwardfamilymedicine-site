## The Internet Makes This Harder Than It Needs to Be

The internet can make environmental health feel impossible.

There are thousands of products marketed as clean, natural, non-toxic, or chemical-free. Some recommendations are useful. Others create unnecessary fear.

I prefer a simpler approach: focus on practical changes that reduce unnecessary exposure without turning your home into a laboratory. This post pulls together the whole series into one list.

## 1. Filter Drinking Water When Appropriate

If your water contains PFAS or other contaminants of concern, choose a filtration system independently certified for the contaminants you want to reduce. I go deeper on this in my post on [PFAS and forever chemicals](/blog/pfas-forever-chemicals-reduce-exposure/).

## 2. Don't Heat Food in Plastic

Use glass or ceramic when microwaving or reheating food.

## 3. Store Food in Glass More Often

Replace frequently used plastic food-storage containers with glass as they wear out.

## 4. Reduce Disposable Bottled Water

Use a reusable stainless-steel or glass bottle for everyday hydration when practical.

## 5. Eat More Whole and Minimally Processed Foods

This is one of the most important recommendations on the list. It supports metabolic health, cardiovascular health, fiber intake, and overall diet quality, and may also reduce opportunities for micro- and nanoplastic exposure associated with extensive food processing and packaging.

## 6. Choose Cookware Thoughtfully

When replacing cookware, consider stainless steel, cast iron, carbon steel, ceramic-coated options, or other products with clearly disclosed materials. My [cookware guide](/blog/choosing-healthier-cookware/) covers the tradeoffs.

## 7. Replace Worn Plastic Cutting Boards

Consider wood or bamboo for many food-preparation tasks, especially as old plastic boards become heavily scored.

## 8. Choose Lower-Plastic Appliances When Replacing Them

For frequently used appliances such as electric kettles or blenders, consider models that minimize plastic contact with hot water or food.

## 9. Reduce Grease-Resistant Food Packaging

PFAS have historically been used in some grease-resistant food-contact materials. Reduce unnecessary reliance on these materials when practical.

## 10. Be Selective With Stain- and Water-Resistant Products

If a product does not need to be stain- or water-resistant, consider whether that treatment provides enough benefit to justify it.

## 11. Choose Fragrance-Free Products When Practical

Fragrance-free laundry, cleaning, and personal-care products can reduce unnecessary exposure to fragrance mixtures.

## 12. Skip Routine Air Fresheners

If you want a fresher home, prioritize ventilation and source control rather than masking odors with fragrance.

## 13. Clean Rather Than Constantly Disinfect

For most routine household situations, cleaning with soap and water is sufficient. Save disinfection for situations where it is actually useful, as I explain in my post on [whether you need to disinfect your house](/blog/do-you-need-to-disinfect-your-house/).

## 14. Consider Your Toothbrush and Floss

Toothbrushes and conventional synthetic floss can contribute to plastic exposure. You do not need to stop brushing or flossing, since the benefits are clear, but when replacing products, you can consider lower-plastic alternatives.

## 15. Replace Things as They Wear Out

You do not need to throw away everything you own.

A sustainable approach is to replace the products you use most often as they wear out. That reduces waste, spreads out the cost, and lets you make better choices over time.

## What I Don't Recommend

I do not recommend trying to eliminate every synthetic material from your home.

I also don't recommend spending large amounts of money replacing products that are functioning perfectly, becoming afraid of ordinary household exposures, or letting environmental concerns distract from the fundamentals of health.

## The Lifestyle Medicine Perspective

A healthier home is only one piece of a healthier life.

The biggest gains still come from eating well, moving regularly, sleeping adequately, maintaining meaningful relationships, managing stress, avoiding tobacco, and minimizing harmful substance use.

Environmental health belongs in that picture, but it should make healthy living more practical, not more frightening.
