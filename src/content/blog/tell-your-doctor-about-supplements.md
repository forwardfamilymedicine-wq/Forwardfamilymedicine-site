Many people take supplements to support sleep, energy, exercise performance, digestion, or healthy aging. Because they are available without a prescription, it is easy to assume they are harmless.

But supplements can have side effects, interact with prescription medications, affect laboratory tests, and complicate treatment decisions. Your physician needs to know what you take, even if you consider it a natural product rather than a medication.

## Natural does not always mean safe

Some supplements have meaningful biological effects. St. John's wort, for example, can interact with numerous medications. Vitamin K intake can affect warfarin management. High doses of certain vitamins and minerals can cause toxicity or interfere with treatment.

The risk depends on the substance, dose, product quality, other medications, and your health conditions.

## Supplements can affect laboratory results

Biotin, often marketed for hair, skin, and nails, can interfere with certain laboratory assays. Depending on the test, this may produce misleading results.

Other supplements may affect liver enzymes, kidney function, blood clotting, or glucose levels.

Tell your clinician and laboratory team about your supplements before testing. Whether you need to pause a product before a particular test depends on the assay and your clinical circumstances.

## Product quality matters

Dietary supplements are not reviewed by the FDA for safety and effectiveness in the same way as prescription medications before they reach the market.

Ingredients, potency, contamination, and labeling accuracy can vary. Independent quality testing may offer additional information about a product, but it does not prove that the supplement works or is appropriate for you.

Be especially cautious of products that promise rapid weight loss, hormonal optimization, detoxification, or treatment of serious diseases without credible evidence.

## How to review your supplement routine

Bring a complete list to your next [primary care visit](/blog/comprehensive-primary-care-visit/). Include:

- The product name and manufacturer.
- The dose and how often you take it.
- Why you started taking it.
- Any benefit or side effect you have noticed.
- Any recent changes to the product or dose.

Include powders, gummies, herbal products, vitamins, minerals, sports supplements, and products recommended by friends or online influencers.

Then discuss which products have a clear purpose, which have evidence supporting their use, and which may be unnecessary or risky.

## A simpler, more thoughtful approach

More supplements do not necessarily mean better health. Correcting a documented deficiency can be valuable, but taking multiple products without a clear indication can increase cost and complexity without improving outcomes.

At Forward Family Medicine, we consider supplements alongside prescription medications, laboratory findings, nutrition, and the patient's goals. The aim is not to dismiss supplements or recommend them indiscriminately. It is to understand which ones are likely to help and which ones deserve reconsideration.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I stop supplements before blood work?

It depends on the product and the tests being performed. Biotin is particularly important to disclose because it can interfere with some assays. Ask your clinician for test-specific instructions.

### Can supplements interact with prescription medications?

Yes. Some interactions can be clinically significant, particularly with anticoagulants, immunosuppressants, and medications with narrow therapeutic ranges.

### Are third-party-tested supplements safe?

Independent testing can help verify certain quality attributes, but it does not guarantee safety, effectiveness, or suitability for your individual medical circumstances.

*Tell your healthcare professional about all supplements you use before changing treatment or beginning a new product.*
