Feeling tired after a demanding week is normal. Feeling exhausted most days, despite making an effort to rest, is different.

Persistent fatigue can affect concentration, exercise tolerance, mood, work, and relationships. Because so many conditions can cause it, the most useful first step is not necessarily a large laboratory panel. It is understanding what the fatigue feels like, when it started, and what else has changed.

## First, distinguish fatigue from sleepiness

Fatigue is a lack of physical or mental energy. Sleepiness is a tendency to fall asleep.

The distinction matters. Someone who struggles to stay awake during the day may need an evaluation for insufficient sleep or a sleep disorder. Someone who sleeps adequately but feels physically depleted may need a different investigation.

Both can occur together.

## Common causes of persistent fatigue

**Sleep problems.** Insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea, restless legs, and inconsistent sleep schedules can all impair daytime function. Loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, morning headaches, and unrefreshing sleep can raise suspicion for sleep apnea.

**Anemia or nutritional deficiencies.** Iron deficiency, vitamin B12 deficiency, and inadequate energy intake can contribute to fatigue in the appropriate clinical setting.

**Thyroid and other medical conditions.** Thyroid disorders, diabetes, chronic inflammatory conditions, and other illnesses can cause persistent changes in energy.

**Medication effects.** Sedating antihistamines, some psychiatric medications, alcohol, and other substances can affect alertness or sleep quality.

**Hormonal transitions.** Perimenopause and menopause can disrupt sleep and affect energy. Other hormonal disorders may also be relevant depending on the symptoms and history.

**Mental health and stress.** Depression, anxiety, chronic stress, and burnout can produce substantial physical fatigue. These causes are not less real because they involve emotional or psychological factors.

## How should fatigue be evaluated?

A good evaluation begins with duration, sleep patterns, recent illnesses, medication changes, diet, activity, menstrual history when relevant, mood, and associated symptoms.

The physical examination and initial tests depend on those findings. A complete blood count, thyroid testing, glucose assessment, iron studies, or other investigations may be appropriate, but not everyone needs all of them.

If the initial evaluation is reassuring, the next step should still be clear. That may involve targeted treatment, additional evaluation, or reassessment over time. ## When should you seek care urgently?

Fatigue accompanied by chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, confusion, significant bleeding, or sudden neurological symptoms requires urgent assessment. New, unexplained, persistent fatigue also deserves medical evaluation rather than being automatically attributed to age or a busy schedule.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can menopause cause fatigue?

Yes. Menopausal symptoms, particularly sleep disruption and night sweats, can contribute to fatigue. Other potential causes should still be considered. Our [menopause care page](/menopause/) explains how we approach it.

### Should I check cortisol or testosterone for fatigue?

These tests are not appropriate for everyone with fatigue. Testing should be based on specific symptoms, clinical findings, and the likelihood that the result will change management.

### How long is too long to feel tired?

There is no single threshold for everyone. Fatigue that persists, worsens, interferes with daily life, or occurs with other concerning symptoms warrants evaluation.
