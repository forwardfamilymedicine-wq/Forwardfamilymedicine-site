## What Are PFAS?

PFAS stands for per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, a large family of synthetic chemicals that have been used because they resist heat, oil, grease, and water.

Some PFAS persist in the environment for a very long time, which is why they are often called forever chemicals.

## Why Should We Care?

Research has associated exposure to certain PFAS with several health outcomes, including changes in cholesterol, immune response, liver enzymes, pregnancy-related complications, and certain cancers.

These findings do not mean that an individual exposure will cause disease. Risk depends on the specific chemical, dose, duration, and individual circumstances, and the science continues to evolve.

## 1. Start With Drinking Water

For many people, drinking water is one of the most actionable sources to address.

If you use a filtration system, look for a product independently certified to reduce PFAS. Reverse osmosis is one effective treatment approach for many contaminants.

If you are on municipal water, check local water-quality information. If you use a private well, testing may be worthwhile depending on your location and circumstances.

## 2. Be Thoughtful About Cookware

PFAS chemistry has historically been used in nonstick cookware and coatings.

If you are replacing cookware, stainless steel, cast iron, carbon steel, or products with clearly disclosed materials can be reasonable choices.

You don't need to panic about a pan you already own. Replacement is a good opportunity to make a more informed choice. I go deeper on this in my [guide to choosing healthier cookware](/blog/choosing-healthier-cookware/).

## 3. Reduce Grease-Resistant Food Packaging

PFAS have historically been used in grease-resistant food-contact materials.

You can reduce unnecessary exposure by choosing fewer foods that spend extensive time in grease-resistant packaging and by transferring takeout or packaged foods to glass or ceramic when practical.

## 4. Think About Stain- and Water-Resistant Products

PFAS have been used in products designed to resist water, oil, and stains.

When buying new carpets, upholstery, outdoor gear, or clothing, consider whether stain or water resistance is actually necessary and look for transparent material information.

## Don't Chase Every Possible Source

PFAS are widespread. Trying to eliminate every possible exposure is neither realistic nor necessary.

Focus on high-yield changes: water filtration when appropriate, less unnecessary grease-resistant packaging, and informed choices when replacing cookware and other treated products.

## Bottom Line

PFAS are a legitimate environmental health concern, but the goal should be practical risk reduction rather than fear.

Make changes that are easy, repeatable, and meaningful, and then move on to the health behaviors with the strongest evidence for improving long-term health.
