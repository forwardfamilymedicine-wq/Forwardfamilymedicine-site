It is easy to feel overwhelmed by headlines about PFAS, microplastics, endocrine disruptors, and chemicals in everyday products. But improving your health does not require creating a perfectly toxic-free home.

A better approach is to focus on exposures that are practical to reduce and to make changes that fit into normal life.

This is the same philosophy I use in Lifestyle Medicine: focus on high-impact behaviors, avoid unnecessary fear, and make sustainable improvements over time. It is the foundation of how I practice at [Forward Family Medicine](/lifestyle-medicine), and it applies just as well to your home environment as it does to nutrition, movement, and sleep.

## 1. Improve the Water You Drink

If you are concerned about PFAS and other contaminants, drinking water is one of the most useful places to start.

If your water source is known to contain PFAS, or if you simply want to reduce exposure, consider a filtration system that has been independently certified to reduce PFAS. Reverse osmosis systems can be particularly effective for many contaminants.

Look for actual third-party certification rather than relying solely on marketing claims.

## 2. Stop Heating Food in Plastic

One of the simplest changes you can make is to avoid heating food in plastic containers.

Transfer leftovers to glass or ceramic before microwaving. You do not need to throw away every plastic container in your kitchen. Simply make this change for the foods you heat most often.

## 3. Choose Cookware Thoughtfully

Traditional nonstick cookware has historically relied on PFAS chemistry. If you are replacing worn cookware, consider stainless steel, cast iron, carbon steel, or newer cookware that clearly states what its coating is made from.

The goal isn't to fear your existing cookware. It is to make a more informed choice when you replace it.

## 4. Reduce Disposable Plastic Contact With Food and Drinks

Reusable glass and stainless-steel containers can reduce reliance on disposable plastics.

Prioritize the items you use every day: water bottles, food-storage containers, and containers used for hot foods or beverages.

## 5. Reduce Fragrance Where It Adds Little Value

Fragrance compounds are found in many household and personal-care products. If you want to reduce unnecessary chemical exposure, consider fragrance-free laundry products, cleaning products, and personal-care products where practical.

This does not mean every scented product is dangerous. It is simply an easy way to reduce the number of unnecessary ingredients in your environment.

## 6. Don't Try to Sterilize Your Home

Routine cleaning is important, but most homes do not need to be continuously disinfected.

For everyday situations, cleaning with soap and water is generally sufficient. Disinfection becomes more useful when someone is sick, after certain types of contamination, or when specifically recommended.

A healthy home is a clean home, not a sterile one.

## The Big Picture

Environmental health matters, but it should complement, not replace, the fundamentals of Lifestyle Medicine.

Prioritize nutritious food, physical activity, sleep, healthy relationships, stress management, avoidance of tobacco, and other well-established health behaviors. Then make practical environmental improvements as opportunities arise.

You don't need a perfect home. You need a home that is a little healthier than it was yesterday.
