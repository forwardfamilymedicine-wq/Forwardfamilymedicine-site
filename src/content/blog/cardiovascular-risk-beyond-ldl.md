If your cholesterol panel looks normal, is your heart disease risk low?

Not necessarily. LDL cholesterol remains an important cardiovascular risk marker, but it is not the entire picture. Depending on your individual risk factors, additional testing can sometimes reveal information that a standard lipid panel does not capture.

The key is knowing which tests can change a decision and which are unlikely to add meaningful value.

## ApoB: How many atherogenic particles are circulating?

Apolipoprotein B, commonly called ApoB, is a protein found on atherogenic lipoprotein particles, including LDL and several other particles that can contribute to plaque formation.

LDL cholesterol measures the amount of cholesterol carried within LDL particles. ApoB provides an estimate of the number of circulating atherogenic particles.

These measurements can be discordant. Someone may have a relatively reassuring LDL cholesterol result but a higher-than-expected ApoB level. ApoB can be particularly useful in selected patients with elevated triglycerides, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, or residual cardiovascular risk.

It does not replace every standard lipid measurement, but it can refine the assessment. We cover the difference in more detail in [ApoB versus LDL](/lifestyle/cardiovascular-prevention/understanding-apob-ldl/).

## Lipoprotein(a): An inherited risk factor

Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), is a largely genetically determined lipoprotein associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and aortic valve stenosis.

Unlike many conventional risk factors, Lp(a) is generally relatively stable throughout adulthood. Measuring it at least once in adulthood is recommended by several major cardiovascular societies, although individual guidelines differ in their specific screening recommendations.

A high Lp(a) result does not mean a cardiovascular event is inevitable. It can, however, strengthen the case for careful management of LDL cholesterol, blood pressure, smoking, and other modifiable risks.

## What about hs-CRP and coronary calcium?

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein, or hs-CRP, measures a marker of systemic inflammation. In selected patients, it can help refine cardiovascular risk assessment.

A coronary artery calcium scan is different. It is an imaging study rather than a blood test. It can help estimate the burden of calcified coronary plaque and may be useful when a preventive treatment decision remains uncertain after a conventional risk assessment.

Neither test is necessary for everyone. Their value depends on age, baseline risk, family history, and whether the result would change management.

## What should you ask your physician to check?

Start with a standard lipid panel, blood pressure, glucose or HbA1c when appropriate, smoking history, and family history of premature cardiovascular disease.

From there, discuss whether ApoB, Lp(a), hs-CRP, or coronary calcium imaging would add useful information.

Advanced testing should answer a specific question, not simply make a laboratory report longer.

## Putting the results together

At Forward Family Medicine, we approach cardiovascular prevention by looking at the full risk profile rather than treating a single cholesterol number in isolation. That includes family history, metabolic health, lifestyle, blood pressure, and the evidence supporting medication when indicated.

The purpose of additional testing is to make better decisions, not to create a longer list of abnormalities.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can ApoB be high when LDL is normal?

Yes. The amount of cholesterol carried by particles and the number of atherogenic particles do not always align.

### Should everyone get an Lp(a) test?

Many contemporary guidelines support measuring it at least once in adulthood. Your physician can interpret the result in the context of your personal and family history.

### Does a high ApoB or Lp(a) mean I need a statin?

Not automatically. Treatment depends on your overall cardiovascular risk, the degree of elevation, other risk factors, and shared decision-making.
