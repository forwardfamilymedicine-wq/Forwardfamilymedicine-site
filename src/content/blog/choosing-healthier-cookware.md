## Why Cookware Gets So Much Attention

Cookware is one of the few things in your home that repeatedly comes into direct contact with food, often at high temperatures.

That makes cookware worth thinking about. But it is also easy to become unnecessarily worried.

The goal is not to find a magical toxin-free pan. It is to choose cookware made from materials you are comfortable using, maintain it appropriately, and replace it when it is worn out.

## Traditional Nonstick Cookware

Traditional nonstick coatings have historically relied on fluorinated chemistry, including PFAS-related materials. If you want the background on why that matters, I cover it in my post on [PFAS and forever chemicals](/blog/pfas-forever-chemicals-reduce-exposure/).

These coatings are convenient, but if you are replacing old cookware, you have many alternatives.

## Stainless Steel

Stainless steel is durable, versatile, and free of a nonstick coating.

It is particularly useful for sauteing, searing, sauces, soups, and many everyday cooking tasks. Learning to preheat the pan and use an appropriate amount of oil can make sticking much less frustrating.

## Cast Iron and Carbon Steel

Cast iron and carbon steel can provide excellent cooking performance and become naturally more nonstick as they are seasoned and maintained.

They are especially useful for high-heat cooking, searing, roasting, and many one-pan meals.

## Ceramic Nonstick

Ceramic-coated cookware can provide a nonstick experience without traditional PTFE-style fluoropolymer coatings. If you choose ceramic nonstick, look for clear manufacturer information about what the coating contains.

As with any coated pan, avoid excessive overheating and replace cookware when the coating is substantially damaged.

## Titanium and Other Modern Options

Some newer cookware products use titanium, stainless steel, or combinations of metals designed to provide a durable cooking surface without a conventional nonstick coating.

The important thing is to look beyond marketing language and identify the actual materials used.

## What I Would Do in My Own Kitchen

You don't need to replace everything at once.

A practical setup might include:

- Stainless steel for everyday cooking.
- Cast iron or carbon steel for high-heat cooking.
- A quality nonstick alternative for foods where easy release matters.
- Glass or ceramic bakeware for the oven.

Replace cookware gradually as it wears out rather than creating unnecessary waste.

## Don't Lose the Forest for the Trees

Cookware matters, but it is not the most important determinant of your health.

The difference between eating vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and fruit regularly versus relying heavily on ultra-processed foods is far more consequential than agonizing over which pan you used to cook dinner.

Choose sensible cookware, and then focus on the meal.
