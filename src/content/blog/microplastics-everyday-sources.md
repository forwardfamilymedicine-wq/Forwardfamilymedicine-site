When people hear microplastics, they often picture plastic bottles or ocean pollution.

But exposure is much closer to home. Microplastics are tiny plastic particles, while nanoplastics are even smaller. Researchers have detected these particles in food, beverages, household dust, and human tissues.

We are still learning what different levels of exposure mean for long-term human health. That makes it important to avoid both complacency and unnecessary fear.

Rather than trying to eliminate every possible exposure, focus on sources you can realistically reduce.

## 1. Processed and Ultra-Processed Foods

This is an area that deserves much more attention.

Processed and ultra-processed foods can have extensive contact with plastic during manufacturing, processing, packaging, storage, and transportation. Micro- and nanoplastics have been detected in a variety of foods and beverages, although the relative contribution of different dietary sources and the health significance of typical exposures remain active areas of research.

This does not mean that eating a packaged food occasionally is going to harm you. But it provides another reason to make whole and minimally processed foods the foundation of your diet.

The less your diet depends on heavily packaged, highly processed foods, the fewer opportunities there are for plastic-related exposure, and the better your diet tends to be overall.

## 2. Heating Food in Plastic

Heat can increase the migration of chemicals and particles from food-contact materials.

My simple rule: don't microwave food in plastic. Transfer leftovers to glass or ceramic before heating them.

## 3. Storing Food in Plastic

Glass containers are particularly useful for leftovers and meal preparation.

You do not need to throw away every plastic container tomorrow. Replace the ones you use most often as they wear out.

## 4. Plastic Water Bottles

Bottled water can contain micro- and nanoplastics. For everyday hydration, a reusable stainless-steel or glass bottle can reduce reliance on disposable plastic bottles.

If you currently drink a lot of bottled water, this is a particularly easy swap.

## 5. Electric Kettles

Some electric kettles contain plastic components that contact hot water.

When replacing a kettle, consider one with a stainless-steel interior and minimal plastic contact with the water.

## 6. Blenders

Some high-powered blenders use plastic containers that contact food during high-speed blending.

If you use a blender frequently, a stainless-steel or glass container can reduce repeated plastic-food contact.

## 7. Cutting Boards

Plastic cutting boards can release plastic particles as the surface wears during cutting.

Wood or bamboo can be useful alternatives for many foods. A plastic board reserved for raw meat can still be practical if it can be thoroughly cleaned.

## 8. Plastic Wrap and Takeout Containers

Glass storage containers, stainless-steel containers, reusable food covers, and other reusable options can reduce disposable plastic use.

The goal is not perfection. It is reducing unnecessary plastic contact with food.

## 9. Toothbrushes

Toothbrushes are primarily plastic, and their bristles undergo mechanical wear during brushing.

The long-term health significance of swallowing particles from toothbrush wear is not established, and the benefits of brushing clearly outweigh the concern. Still, when replacing a toothbrush, you can consider options with replaceable heads, less plastic overall, or recyclable components where available.

Do not stop brushing your teeth to avoid plastic exposure.

## 10. Dental Floss

Many conventional dental flosses are made from synthetic polymers and may include additional coatings.

Good oral hygiene is far more important than eliminating every possible plastic exposure. But if reducing unnecessary plastic is a priority, you can explore lower-plastic alternatives such as silk floss or other materials.

## The Bigger Picture

My priorities would be:

- Eat mostly whole or minimally processed foods.
- Don't heat food in plastic.
- Use glass or stainless steel for frequently stored foods.
- Reduce reliance on disposable plastic bottles.
- Choose lower-plastic food-preparation tools when replacing them.
- Consider plastic exposure from everyday items such as toothbrushes and floss.

Most importantly, don't let concern about microplastics distract you from the things with much stronger evidence for improving health: nutritious food, exercise, sleep, healthy relationships, stress management, and avoidance of tobacco.

Environmental exposure reduction should complement Lifestyle Medicine, not replace it.
