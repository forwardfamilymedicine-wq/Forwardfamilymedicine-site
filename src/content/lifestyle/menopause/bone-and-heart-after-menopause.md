Hot flashes get the attention because they are impossible to ignore. But the changes that most affect how a woman ages happen silently, in the years after the final period, in two places she cannot feel: her bones and her arteries.

Estrogen does more than regulate the reproductive cycle. It slows the breakdown of bone. It keeps blood vessels flexible, helps maintain a favorable lipid profile, and influences where the body stores fat. When estrogen falls at menopause, each of those protections fades, and the effects compound over the following decade.

That decade is the window. What happens in the ten years after menopause shapes the thirty that follow.

## Bone

Bone is living tissue, constantly being broken down and rebuilt. Before menopause, estrogen holds that balance in favor of building. After menopause, breakdown outpaces rebuilding, and the loss is fastest in the first several years after the final period. A woman can lose a meaningful fraction of her bone density in that early window before the rate slows.

The consequence is osteoporosis, and the consequence of osteoporosis is fracture. Hip and spine fractures in older women are not minor events. They change independence, mobility, and life expectancy. The best time to prevent them is decades earlier, in exactly the years when bone is being lost fastest and nothing feels wrong.

**What to do about it.**

Know your baseline. A DEXA scan measures bone density and is the standard screening test. Routine screening begins at sixty-five, but that guideline is a floor, not a ceiling. Women with risk factors, including early menopause, a family history of osteoporosis or hip fracture, low body weight, a history of steroid use, or certain medical conditions, should be screened earlier. Many women benefit from a baseline scan around the time of menopause so that change can be tracked. Forward Family Medicine's [Wayne DEXA](/wayne-dexa/) service offers this on site.

Load the skeleton. Bone responds to force. Resistance training, meaning lifting weight heavy enough to be challenging, is the most effective exercise for preserving and building bone density. Walking is good for many things but is not enough on its own. [Strength training](/lifestyle/physical-activity/strength-training-metabolic-health/) two to three times a week, progressing over time, is the standard recommendation and is worth taking seriously.

Feed it. Adequate protein, adequate calcium from food where possible, and vitamin D sufficiency are the basics. These do not build bone on their own, but bone cannot be built without them.

Consider hormone therapy. Estrogen is highly effective at preventing postmenopausal bone loss and reducing fracture risk, and for women who are candidates on other grounds, this is one of its clearest benefits. It is not usually started for bone alone, but it counts in the decision.

Treat when indicated. For women with osteoporosis, or with low bone density and high fracture risk, medications exist that reduce fracture rates substantially. These are underused, largely because the condition is silent until something breaks.

## Heart

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in women. That is not widely understood, partly because heart disease is still thought of as a men's problem, and partly because women tend to develop it a decade later than men, which is to say after menopause.

The pattern is consistent. In the years following menopause, LDL cholesterol and ApoB rise. Blood pressure tends to climb. Fat redistributes toward the abdomen, which is the pattern most associated with metabolic risk. Insulin sensitivity declines. None of this is inevitable, but all of it is common, and it happens quietly.

**What to do about it.**

Measure the right things. A standard lipid panel is a starting point. Adding ApoB gives a more accurate picture of the particles that actually cause arterial disease, and it is worth having at least once around the time of menopause. [What ApoB measures and why it matters](/lifestyle/cardiovascular-prevention/understanding-apob-ldl/) is covered in its own article. Lipoprotein(a), a genetically determined risk factor, should be checked once in every adult and is often missed. Blood pressure at every visit. Glucose or A1c. Waist measurement, not just weight.

Address what the numbers show. Elevated ApoB or LDL responds to dietary change, to exercise, and when those are not enough, to medication. The threshold for treatment depends on overall risk, and overall risk in a postmenopausal woman is often higher than she or her physician assumes. Blood pressure that has drifted into the elevated range is worth treating rather than watching.

Move in the right ways. Aerobic exercise improves cardiovascular fitness, blood pressure, and insulin sensitivity. Resistance training, already essential for bone, also improves metabolic health. The combination is the goal, and it does not require a gym membership or a large time commitment to start.

Understand hormone therapy's role. This is where the evidence is most nuanced. Hormone therapy is not prescribed to prevent heart disease, and it should not be started for that reason. But the older belief that it causes heart disease in healthy women has not held up when therapy begins under sixty or within ten years of menopause. For a woman who is a candidate on other grounds, the cardiovascular picture is one input into the decision, not a reason to avoid it. [The 2026 evidence on hormone therapy](/lifestyle/menopause/hormone-therapy-evidence-2026/) covers this in more detail.
