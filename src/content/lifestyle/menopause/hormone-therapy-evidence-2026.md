For most of the past two decades, the conversation about hormone therapy for menopause started from fear. Many women were told it caused breast cancer and heart attacks. Many physicians stopped prescribing it. A generation went through menopause managing symptoms with whatever was left.

In February 2026, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved label changes removing cardiovascular disease, breast cancer, and probable dementia from the boxed warning on the first group of menopausal hormone therapy products. The decision followed an expert advisory panel in July 2025 and a formal announcement that November that the agency would initiate the change. That is the strongest safety warning the agency has, and it had sat on these medications since 2003.

This is worth understanding in some detail, because the story of how the warning got there, and why it came off, is the story of how to think about hormone therapy now.

## Where the fear came from

In 2002, the Women's Health Initiative, a large randomized trial, stopped its estrogen-plus-progestin arm early after finding increased rates of breast cancer, heart disease, stroke, and blood clots among women taking the combination compared with placebo. The findings were reported as applying to hormone therapy in general, prescriptions fell by roughly half within a year, and the boxed warning followed.

What was less widely reported was who the participants were. The average age at enrollment was 63. Most were more than ten years past their last period. Many had never had menopausal symptoms and were taking hormones for prevention rather than treatment. The trial studied one specific oral formulation at one dose. It was designed to test whether hormone therapy prevented heart disease in older women, not whether it was safe for symptomatic women in their fifties.

When researchers later separated the results by age, a different picture emerged. Women who started therapy under sixty, or within ten years of menopause, did not show the cardiovascular harm seen in the older group, and some analyses suggested a benefit. The risks that dominated the headlines were concentrated among women who started much later. This became known as the timing hypothesis, and it has been reinforced by follow-up data and by other trials since.

## What the evidence supports today

The Menopause Society's position, restated in its 2022 hormone therapy statement, is that for healthy women who are under sixty or within ten years of their final period and have bothersome symptoms, the benefits of hormone therapy generally outweigh the risks. That position is now reflected in the FDA's labeling. It is not a fringe view. It is the mainstream clinical consensus.

Hormone therapy remains the most effective treatment available for hot flashes and night sweats. It treats genitourinary symptoms. It prevents the bone loss that accelerates after menopause and reduces fracture risk. Depending on the woman and the timing, it may improve sleep, mood, and joint symptoms as well.

## What did not change

Removing a boxed warning is not the same as declaring a medication risk-free, and the label revision was careful about that.

**Blood clots and stroke.** Oral estrogen raises the risk of venous thromboembolism and, modestly, of stroke. These risks are small in absolute terms for a healthy woman in her fifties, but they are real. Estrogen delivered through the skin, as a patch, gel, or spray, does not pass through the liver first and appears to carry lower clot and stroke risk than the oral route. For most women starting therapy today, transdermal is the default.

**Breast cancer.** The increased risk seen in the WHI was with combined estrogen and progestin, emerged after several years of use, and was small: on the order of fewer than one additional case per thousand women per year in the trial. Estrogen alone, in women who have had a hysterectomy, did not show that increase. The type of progestogen may matter. Micronized progesterone, which is chemically identical to the body's own, appears to carry less risk than the synthetic progestin used in the WHI, though the data are observational.

**The uterus.** Estrogen given alone to a woman who still has her uterus raises the risk of endometrial cancer. That warning stays on the label, and it is why estrogen is paired with a progestogen unless the uterus has been removed.

**Who should not take it.** A personal history of breast cancer or other hormone-sensitive cancer, a history of blood clots or stroke, active liver disease, and unexplained vaginal bleeding are among the reasons hormone therapy may not be appropriate. Each of these is a conversation rather than a closed door, but they change the calculation.

## How the decision is actually made

The question is never "is hormone therapy safe." It is "is hormone therapy the right choice for this woman, at this point in her transition, given her symptoms, her history, and what she wants."

That conversation covers timing: how long since the last period, or where in perimenopause. Symptoms: what is bothersome enough to treat, and what else might explain it. Personal and family history: breast cancer, clots, heart disease, osteoporosis. Cardiovascular risk: blood pressure, lipids including [ApoB](/lifestyle/cardiovascular-prevention/understanding-apob-ldl/), glucose. Preferences: some women want to try hormones, some want every non-hormonal option first, and both are reasonable.

It also covers the alternatives, because hormone therapy is one option among several. Non-hormonal medications, including certain antidepressants at low doses, gabapentin, and fezolinetant, a newer drug that acts on the brain's temperature regulation, all reduce hot flashes. Fezolinetant carries its own boxed warning for liver injury and requires liver enzyme testing before starting and periodically during treatment, which is part of the conversation about whether it is the right fit. Low-dose vaginal estrogen treats genitourinary symptoms with minimal absorption into the bloodstream and is appropriate for many women who cannot or prefer not to use systemic hormones. Lifestyle measures matter for sleep, weight, mood, and long-term bone and heart health regardless of what else is chosen.

Then there is the question of how long. The old guidance to use the lowest dose for the shortest time has been replaced. Duration is individualized, reassessed regularly, and can extend well beyond five years when the benefits continue to outweigh the risks for that woman.

## Why this takes more than fifteen minutes

Every element above is a judgment call informed by evidence, and the evidence has shifted enough in twenty years that a physician trained before 2010 and not retrained since may be working from a picture that no longer holds. The Menopause Society Certified Practitioner credential exists partly for that reason: it requires demonstrated competency in the current evidence, not the evidence as it stood in 2002.
