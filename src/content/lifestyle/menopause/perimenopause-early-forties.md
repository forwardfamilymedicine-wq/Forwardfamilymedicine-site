Most women expect menopause to announce itself. Hot flashes, a period that stops, done. What actually happens is quieter and starts earlier than almost anyone is told.

Perimenopause, the transition leading up to the final period, commonly begins in the mid-forties and sometimes in the late thirties. It lasts around four years on average and can run closer to a decade. During that time, the ovaries do not wind down smoothly. They sputter. Estrogen and progesterone swing unpredictably from cycle to cycle, sometimes higher than they have been in years, sometimes far lower, and the body reacts to the swings as much as to the overall decline.

That is why the earliest symptoms are so easy to miss. They rarely look like a hormone problem. They look like stress, or [poor sleep](/lifestyle/sleep-hormones/sleep-and-hormone-regulation/), or getting older, or the year everything at work got harder.

## The symptoms that get attributed to everything else

**Sleep changes.** Waking at three or four in the morning and not getting back to sleep is one of the most common early signs, and it often appears before any change in periods. It gets blamed on anxiety, caffeine, or a busy mind. Sometimes those are factors. Often the underlying driver is a shift in progesterone, which has a sedating effect that fades as levels fall.

**Mood that feels unfamiliar.** Irritability with a short fuse, anxiety that arrives without a clear cause, a low mood that does not match your circumstances. Women who have never had mood symptoms describe feeling like a different person. Women who have a history of premenstrual mood changes or postpartum depression tend to be more sensitive to the hormonal swings of perimenopause and may notice this earlier.

**Cognitive fog.** Losing words mid-sentence, walking into rooms and forgetting why, struggling to hold a train of thought through an interruption. This is common, it is real, and for most women it improves after the transition. It is not early dementia, though the fear of that is common too.

**Cycle changes that do not look like "stopping."** Cycles may shorten to 21 to 24 days before they lengthen. Bleeding may get heavier for a stretch, sometimes much heavier, before it gets lighter. Periods may be skipped and then return. A woman still having regular periods can be well into perimenopause.

**Joint and muscle aches.** New stiffness in the morning, aching hands or shoulders, a feeling of being sore without having done anything. Estrogen has anti-inflammatory effects on joints and connective tissue, and its decline shows up here in ways most people never connect to hormones.

**Weight that redistributes.** Not necessarily a change on the scale, but a change in where weight sits. Fat shifts toward the midsection, and the same eating and exercise that held steady for years stops holding.

**Heart palpitations.** A fluttering or racing heart, often at night or during a hot flash. Usually benign in this context, but it deserves evaluation rather than reassurance alone, because the same symptom has other causes that need ruling out.

**Changes below the waist.** Vaginal dryness, discomfort with intimacy, more frequent urinary tract infections, urgency. These belong to a condition called genitourinary syndrome of menopause, they tend to progress rather than resolve on their own, and they respond well to treatment that most women are never offered.

**New or changing headaches.** Migraines can appear for the first time or change pattern, tracking the hormonal fluctuation of the cycle.

None of these on its own points to perimenopause. Taken together, in a woman in her forties, they form a pattern that has a name and a plan.

## Why hormone testing does not settle it

The instinct is to check hormone levels. In perimenopause that rarely helps and often misleads.

Follicle-stimulating hormone, or FSH, rises as ovarian function declines, and it is the test most often ordered. But in perimenopause, FSH swings dramatically from week to week. A single normal value does not rule out perimenopause. A single elevated value does not confirm it. Estradiol is the same. Levels drawn on a good week look fine, levels drawn on a bad week look alarming, and neither tells you much about the month as a whole.

The Menopause Society and other major bodies are clear on this: for women over forty-five with typical symptoms, the diagnosis of perimenopause is made on the history, not on a blood test. Labs still have a role. Thyroid function, iron studies if bleeding is heavy, and screening for other conditions that mimic these symptoms are all worth doing. But "your hormones are normal" is not a reason to be sent home without a plan.

## Why this gets missed in a fifteen-minute visit

The symptoms above touch sleep, mood, cognition, gynecology, musculoskeletal health, cardiology, and urology. In most primary care settings, each is handled as its own complaint in its own visit. The sleep problem gets a sleep hygiene handout. The mood gets an antidepressant. The joints get an anti-inflammatory. The palpitations get a cardiology referral. Nobody is asked to hold all of it in view at once, and the pattern that would have been obvious across a longer conversation never surfaces.

A woman in her mid-forties who reports four or five of the symptoms above should be asked one question first: where is she in the menopausal transition? That question changes everything that follows.

## When to have the conversation

Earlier than you think. If you are in your forties and noticing changes that feel different from your baseline, particularly sleep, mood, cycle pattern, or a cluster of the symptoms above, that is the right time. Not when periods stop. Not when the hot flashes become unbearable. The transition is easier to manage from the front than from the middle, and some of the most consequential changes of menopause, to bone and cardiovascular health, begin during perimenopause rather than after it.

The conversation should be with a clinician who has trained for it and has the time to do it properly.
